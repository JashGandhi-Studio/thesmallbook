Why Fish Don't Exist - Summary & Key Lessons

by -Yo Ma · 2020 · Nature & Planet · ⏱ 8 min read · 8 lessons

A personal meditation on chaos, order, and a nineteenth century ichthyologist whose categories collapsed.

💡 The Big Idea

-Yo Ma, a science reporter, became obsessed with David Starr Jordan, Stanford's first president, who spent his life ordering the chaos of fish into categories, even as earthquakes and lightning destroyed his collections. Interleaved with her own story of depression and a life that would not stay in its categories, the book arrives at taxonomy's modern verdict: fish, as a class, do not exist; the category was ours, not nature's. Its wisdom is about letting the world's real structure correct our filing systems, in science and in a life.

🧠 The 8 Key Lessons

Lesson 1: The Man Who Kept Naming Through Every Disaster

David Starr Jordan's Collections

David Starr Jordan's specimens were destroyed by lightning, fire and the 1906 earthquake, which shattered his life's jars in a single morning. Each time he rebuilt, re-collected, re-labelled, eventually sewing name tags directly onto fish so no disaster could separate them again. -Yo Ma reads his persistence with admiration and unease: the same will that rebuilds a science can harden into the refusal to admit the whole scheme is wrong. Both readings become necessary as his story darkens.

📖 Example: After the earthquake he walked the ruined museum identifying fish on the floor from memory, an act of heroic scholarship and, in -Yo Ma's telling, a warning. The labels went onto the bodies themselves thereafter: order would survive the next disaster by being stitched to the world. Read the full example →

Lesson 2: Fish Do Not Exist, and That Is Good News

The Taxonomic Verdict

Modern cladistics shows a lungfish is closer kin to a cow than to a salmon; the class fish groups creatures by our convenience, water, fins, scales, not by their actual family tree. -Yo Ma receives this personally: the categories that organised her suffering, success and failure, normal life, broken person, were also conveniences mistaken for facts. Dissolving fish becomes a permission slip: revise the taxonomy of your own life when it stops matching the specimens in front of you.

📖 Example: She describes telling people at parties that fish do not exist and watching the productive discomfort spread. The discomfort is the book in miniature: a filing error corrected feels like loss before it feels like freedom, in ichthyology and in biography alike. Read the full example →

⚡ Do this: Write down one label you live under that no longer matches the specimen. Draft its replacement in pencil.

Lesson 3: David Starr Jordan's Shadow: Order in Service of Cruelty

The Eugenics Chapters

-Yo Ma does not let her hero keep his halo: David Starr Jordan was a leading eugenicist whose categories of human fitness fed real policy and real suffering. The book's hardest argument follows: the drive to order is not innocent; misapplied to people it becomes a machinery of ranking. The same man who sewed labels onto fish to defeat chaos helped label human beings to defeat mercy. Remembering both is the reader's assignment, and the reason the book belongs in science and ethics courses alike.

📖 Example: She visits the archives and finds his handwriting on reports that shaped sterilisation laws, in the same hand that labelled specimens with such care. The juxtaposition is the book's moral centre: reverence for order, unchecked by humility about categories, scales from jars to atrocities without changing its handwriting. Read the full example →

⚡ Do this: Audit one ranking you participate in, at work or online, for what it does to the bottom of the list. Change one practice this month.

Lesson 4: The Collector's High and Its Price

Obsession Examined

-Yo Ma examines her own obsession with David Starr Jordan as honestly as his with fish: research as shelter, a story ordered enough to hide inside while a life outside the archive felt unliveable. The book's doubling is its method: two people a century apart using categories to hold back chaos, one with jars, one with a biography. Readers are invited to locate their own jars early, because the book will ask what they are sheltering, and whether the shelter has become the address.

📖 Example: She describes the months in the Stanford archives as among the calmest of her depression, the finding aids and labelled boxes a world that stayed filed. The recognition that the archive was functioning as medication, printed without self pity, is why readers in similar weather trust the book's later steadiness. Read the full example →

⚡ Do this: Name your archive: the orderly project that shelters you. Ask this week whether it is shelter or address, and adjust one hour accordingly.

Lesson 5: Resilience, Reconsidered as Revision

Rebuilding After the Earthquake

David Starr Jordan rebuilt his collections after each disaster, and the culture applauds; -Yo Ma complicates the applause by asking what he never rebuilt, his assumptions. The earthquakes destroyed specimens but never once his taxonomy, and the taxonomy was the error. Her counter definition of resilience, drawn from the fish verdict, is revision: not restoring the shelf exactly, but letting catastrophe edit the categories themselves. Lives, like sciences, break less from disasters than from rebuilding the wrong order faster each time.

📖 Example: She contrasts David Starr Jordan sewing labels to fish with the taxonomists who eventually dissolved fish altogether: the first is endurance, the second is courage, and only the second corrected anything. Couples, companies and countries in her closing pages are audited by which of the two they practise after their earthquakes. Read the full example →

⚡ Do this: After your next setback, before rebuilding, hold a one hour revision: which category of mine did this event falsify? Edit that first.

Lesson 6: The Order of Things Is a Choice With Consequences

Categories and Power

The book's final argument widens from taxonomy to every system that sorts: the categories we build decide what can be seen, funded, forgiven or exterminated. David Starr Jordan's eugenics was not a separate hobby from his ichthyology; it was the same filing cabinet opened on people. -Yo Ma closes by urging a clerical humility: build categories, use them, and hold them the way scientists now hold fish, as conveniences under permanent review, especially wherever the filing is done on human beings.

📖 Example: She returns to the lungfish and the cow, cousins filed in separate universes for centuries by people who trusted water and fins. The absurdity is gentle; the human versions are not, and her last chapters list them quietly: the forms, the boxes, the rankings that decide whose life is legible to the state. ⚡ Do this: Inspect one form, rubric or ranking you administer for a category that no longer matches reality. Propose its revision in writing this month.

Lesson 7: Swimming in Chaos Without Drowning

The Close

-Yo Ma ends not with a system but with a stance: the world will not hold still for our labels, people will not stay in the jars of identity we assign them or ourselves, and a life can be built for the water as it is. Her own closing pages describe a steadiness assembled from exactly this surrender, relationships and work refiled under verbs rather than nouns. It is as close as the book comes to a sermon, and it has been read at more than one wedding and commencement, categories dissolving in public.

📖 Example: She tells graduates, in the version of the argument that went viral, that the fish not existing freed her from needing her life to exist as a recognisable category either. The line travels because everyone listening has a jar with their name on it, and has begun to suspect the label was sewn on by somebody else. Read the full example →

⚡ Do this: Refiling exercise: describe your life this week in verbs only, no identity nouns. Notice which nouns you grieve and which you do not.

Lesson 8: Chaos Is Not the Opposite of Meaning; It Is the Medium

The Order of Things

Miller's conclusion refuses both available consolations: the old order was fiction, yet the fiction held a suffering man upright for decades, and its collapse did not free everyone it touched. Her answer is not a better category but a stance, swimming, she calls it, participating in a world that will not hold still for naming. Deepening means practising taxonomy humility everywhere: your personality type, your child's label, your industry's categories, useful nets, none of them the water. The fish were never in the ocean. They were in us, and we may put them down gently.

📖 Example: Her reporting includes taxonomists who cheerfully confirm the title: fish is a term of convenience, and a salmon is closer kin to you than to a shark. Science survives the admission beautifully, which is the book's deepest comfort. Losing the category loses nothing real; it only returns the wonder the drawer had filed away. Read the full example →

⚡ Do this: Take one label you live inside, your type, diagnosis, role or brand, and write for ten minutes about what you are when it is removed. File under water, not drawer.

✅ 5-Step Action Plan

  1. Review one filing system you keep repairing instead of questioning.
  2. Retire one personal label that fails the specimen test.
  3. Tell someone this week that fish do not exist; enjoy the discomfort.
  4. Audit one ranking you join for its effect on the bottom.
  5. Hold categories as tools: useful, revisable, never sacred.
  6. When chaos ruins your jars, relabel once, then ask if the shelf itself is wrong.
  7. Read it with a pencil; the margins are where your taxonomy lives.

⚠️ When This Doesn't Work

-Yo Ma writes memoir braided with biography, and scientists debate some emphases. The eugenics history is documented and grim reading. Readers in the middle of their own collapsed categories should know the book ends in hard won steadiness, not despair.
