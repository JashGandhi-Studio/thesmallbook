Contagious — Summary & Key Lessons

Why things catch on — the six STEPPS behind every product, idea, and video that spreads.

💡 The Big Idea

Only 7% of word of mouth happens online — but ALL of it follows the same psychology. Berger's Wharton research distilled six principles (STEPPS): SOCIAL CURRENCY (we share what makes us look good — remarkable facts, insider access, visible achievement); TRIGGERS (top of mind = tip of tongue: link your product to frequent environmental cues — Kit Kat wired itself to coffee breaks and revived a dying brand); EMOTION (high-arousal feelings — awe, anger, excitement, anxiety — drive sharing; sadness and contentment kill it: 'when we care, we share'); PUBLIC (visible things get imitated — the Livestrong wristband beat private donations because everyone could SEE it); PRACTICAL VALUE (useful news is generosity — people share deals and how-tos to help others, so package your expertise into shareable utility); STORIES (information travels inside narratives like the Trojan Horse — but the product must be INTEGRAL to the story, not decoration). The liberating conclusion: boring products can be made contagious — a blender, a sandwich, a bar hidden behind a hot-dog shop — because contagiousness lives in the message design, not the product category.

🧠 The 6 Key Lessons

Lesson 1: Social Currency + Triggers: Make Them Look Good, Then Keep Reminding Them

Chapters 1-2

People share things that upgrade their image — remarkable facts ('a blender that shreds iPhones'), game mechanics (status tiers, streaks), and insider scarcity (secret menus, invite-only access all convert exclusivity into evangelism). But image only starts the fire; TRIGGERS keep it burning: we talk about whatever the environment reminds us of, so the growth question is 'what everyday cue can own this?' Frequency beats cleverness — a mediocre association to a daily cue (coffee, Fridays, rain) outperforms a brilliant one to a rare cue. Design the link explicitly and repeat it until the cue does your marketing.

⚡ Do this: Write your product's remarkable fact (the sentence people would repeat at dinner) — if none exists, build one feature/stat worth repeating. Then pick one daily-frequency cue in your customers' lives and link every piece of content to it for 90 days.

Lesson 2: Emotion + Public: High Arousal, High Visibility

Chapters 3-4

Sharing is physiological: high-AROUSAL emotions (awe, excitement, amusement, anger, anxiety) activate us to act and share; low-arousal states (contentment, sadness) sedate us into silence — which is why outrage and wonder dominate feeds while 'satisfied customer' testimonials die quietly. Audit your content: does it light a fire or file a report? Then make the behavior PUBLIC: humans imitate what they can SEE (social proof needs visibility to function), so design self-advertising products (distinctive packaging, visible-in-use design) and behavioral residue (stickers, badges, shareable artifacts that keep advertising after the moment passes).

⚡ Do this: Take your most important message and re-emotionalize it: find the angle that produces awe, amusement, or productive anger (not mild interest). Then add one visibility mechanic: something customers display, wear, share, or that's recognizable from across a room.

Lesson 3: Practical Value + Stories: Useful Trojan Horses

Chapters 5-6

PRACTICAL VALUE is the most underrated share-driver: people forward deals, warnings and how-tos as acts of care ('saw this and thought of you'). Package expertise into tight, forwardable units — one tip per piece, clear headline, obvious beneficiary — and understand deal psychology (the Rule of 100: under $100, percentage discounts feel bigger; over $100, absolute amounts do). STORIES are the delivery vehicle: humans think in narratives, and information hitchhikes inside them — but the test is whether your product is INTEGRAL: retell the story without the brand, and if nothing breaks, you've built entertainment, not marketing. Design stories where the product is the hero's tool, impossible to remove.

⚡ Do this: Create one 'forwardable unit' weekly: a single genuinely useful tip in your domain, formatted to be sent to exactly one person ('send this to a friend who...'). And run the Trojan test on your brand story: remove the product — if the story survives, rebuild it until it can't.

Lesson 4: Running the STEPPS Audit: Engineering Word of Mouth for Boring Products

Epilogue: The Checklist in Action

Berger's closing argument: virality is a CHECKLIST discipline, not a lightning strike — and it works best on 'boring' products, because the bar for surprise is lowest where nobody expects a story. The audit: run every launch, post, or campaign through all six STEPPS as questions — does sharing this make someone look smart/insider (Social currency)? What daily cue will re-trigger it (Trigger)? Which high-arousal emotion does it fire (Emotion)? Can anyone SEE others using it (Public)? Would someone forward it to help a friend (Practical value)? Is the product locked inside the narrative (Story)? Most marketing scores 1 of 6 — a single fix to the weakest dimension routinely outperforms doubling the budget on the strongest. Berger's meta-rule: word of mouth is 10x more effective than paid media BUT only 7% of it happens online — design for the dinner-table retelling, not the retweet, and the retweets follow.

⚡ Do this: Take your current main offer and score it 0-1 on each of the six STEPPS (be harsh). For your lowest dimension, ship one fix this month: add the remarkable fact (social currency), tie content to a daily cue (trigger), re-cut the message for awe or amusement (emotion), make usage visible (public), package one genuinely useful freebie (practical value), or rebuild the story so your product can't be removed from it.

Lesson 5: The Triggers: Keep Reminding Them — and Fit Into Their Lives

Part 2: The STEPPS

Berger's second ingredient: triggers — cues in the environment that make people think of your product or idea. The lesson is counterintuitive: you don't want people to remember you constantly; you want them to remember you at the moment it's relevant — and that's engineered by linking your product to frequent triggers in daily life. The classic case: KitKat and coffee ('Have a break') — the coffee break, happening daily for millions, became the trigger that made KitKat the natural purchase. The strategy: find what your audience does often (morning coffee, commute, lunch) and attach your idea to it, so that every repetition of the trigger re-fires your brand. Triggers beat ads because they work at the moment of decision.

📖 Example: Berger's research showed how a 'Friday' association boosted mentions of a restaurant chain — simply by tying itself to the weekly trigger, the brand got re-shared every single Friday without spending a rupee more on advertising. The trigger did the reminding. Read the full example →

⚡ Do this: Identify one frequent daily habit of your audience. Design one message, product ritual or reminder that links your brand to that moment — and test it this month.

Lesson 6: The Story: Make Your Message a Trojan Horse

Part 5: The STEPPS

Berger's final ingredient: stories are the Trojan horses of ideas — people share stories for their own sake (entertainment, emotion, identity), and your message rides inside them. The viral story isn't the one that mentions your product the most; it's the one people WANT to retell, with your brand woven into the plot so it can't be removed without breaking the tale. The test: if you can delete your product from the story and it still works, your story won't carry your message. The craft is building narratives where the product is integral — the moral, the plot twist, the resolution. When people retell the story, they re-sell your idea, free of charge, forever.

📖 Example: Berger dissects how the viral story of a bar that gave away free food on a 'fake' holiday carried the brand's message inside a tale people wanted to tell — the retelling was the advertising, and the product was the plot. Read the full example →

⚡ Do this: Write your product's 'Trojan horse' story this week: a short, retellable tale where your product is essential to the plot. Test it on one friend and see if they retell it.

✅ 5-Step Action Plan

  1. Engineer one remarkable, repeatable fact about your product — social currency on tap.
  2. Own a high-frequency trigger: link your brand to a daily cue for 90 days.
  3. Re-cut every message for high-arousal emotion; retire the mildly pleasant.
  4. Add visibility mechanics: self-advertising design + behavioral residue.
  5. Ship weekly practical-value units and stories your product can't be removed from.

⚠️ When This Doesn't Work

Berger's virality framework is brilliant — and Vine is its graveyard: the most contagious product of its era, six-second videos that launched a thousand careers and defined internet culture — and the platform died anyway because virality is not a business model. Contagious content attracts attention; it doesn't attract revenue, retention or protection from a parent company's strategy shift. The book can make you chase the share; the graveyard is full of products everyone shared and nobody paid for.
