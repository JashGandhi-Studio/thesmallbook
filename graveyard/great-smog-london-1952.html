Why Did The Great Smog of London, 1952 Fail?

Five Days the City Could Not Breathe

1952YEAR
HISTORYCAUSE
Thousands dead in five days of manufactured fogTHE BURN

What happened

From 5 to 9 December 1952, a cold anticyclone settled over London and trapped the smoke of a coal burning city under an inversion layer. The result was a yellow black fog so dense that visibility fell to metres, transport stopped, a performance at Sadler's Wells was abandoned because the audience could not see the stage, and cattle at Smithfield market suffocated. Londoners, accustomed to pea soupers, initially treated it as weather. The death registers disagreed: about 4,000 excess deaths were recorded in the immediate weeks, and later research raised the toll to roughly 12,000, with perhaps 100,000 people made ill, the elderly, infants and those with heart and lung disease dying at home and in overwhelmed hospitals. Coal was not an industry choice but a household one: millions of domestic fires burning cheap, sulphurous coal, defended as the poor man's warmth against smokeless fuel prices. The government first blamed influenza. Within four years, however, the disaster produced the Clean Air Act of 1956, smoke control areas and the conversion of cities to smokeless fuels and gas, one of the most successful environmental laws of the century, passed because the evidence had been delivered, fatally, to every doorstep in the capital at once.

The fatal mistake

The disaster was a slow subsidy reaching its invoice: cheap coal's real price had been externalised into lungs for a century, and the fog merely concentrated the arithmetic into five visible days. Officialdom's first instinct was narrative management, an influenza epidemic, rather than the measurement sitting in its own registrars' books. Household behaviour was then blamed where infrastructure was the cage: families burned what was cheap because clean fuel cost more, a pricing decision made far above the fireplace. And London had been warned by centuries of fogs and a 1948 disaster in Donora, Pennsylvania, that killed 20: the knowledge existed; only the coalition to act on it was missing, until the smog built one out of mourners.

The lesson

Externalities do not vanish; they accumulate until they become weather. Price the pollution or the lungs settle the account, and they settle it regressively, on the old, the young and the poor first. When the official story and the death register disagree, believe the register and ask who benefits from the story. Clean air in London was not achieved by exhorting households but by changing what fuel cost and what chimneys were legal: infrastructure and prices, not sermons, clear skies. Finally, disasters that touch everyone at once legislate fastest; the task of policy in ordinary times is to act without requiring one.
