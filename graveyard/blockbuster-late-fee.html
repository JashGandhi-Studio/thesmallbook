Why Did Blockbuster and the Offer It Laughed At Fail?

The Late Fee That Ate an Empire

2010YEAR
BUSINESSCAUSE
9,000 stores and 60 million members, gone in a decadeTHE BURN

What happened

In 2000, Blockbuster was a 9 billion dollar empire of some 9,000 stores whose profits ran on late fees, and Netflix was a two year old startup losing money renting DVDs by post. Reed Hastings and Marc Randolph flew to Dallas and offered to sell Netflix to Blockbuster for 50 million dollars. By the principals' own accounts, the Blockbuster side struggled not to laugh. Blockbuster declined, launched its own online service four years later, hobbled it to protect store economics, briefly fought Netflix to real effect under a CEO who understood the threat, then ousted him in a dispute partly about his pay, and retreated. Netflix, meanwhile, survived the dot com crash, shipped streaming in 2007 the moment broadband allowed, and treated the DVD business Blockbuster had mocked as a bridge to burn behind itself. Blockbuster filed for bankruptcy in September 2010 with about a billion dollars of debt, and its remnants were sold to Dish Network. One store in Bend, Oregon, survives as a tourist attraction. The autopsy matters because Blockbuster was not stupid or lazy: it was profitable, and its profits were the problem, a classic case of a business unable to eat its own lunch before a hungrier rival ate it instead.

The fatal mistake

Blockbuster's model was the disease: late fees, a large share of revenue, were a tax on its best customers that Netflix's no late fee subscription was designed to forgive. Protecting store economics delayed and crippled every digital response; the online service that finally worked, Total Access, was undermined from inside by franchise and store interests. The 2000 meeting is remembered for the laugh, but the deeper failure was analytical: Blockbuster priced Netflix as a DVD queue when it was buying a relationship with the living room. And the one executive who fought effectively, John Antioco, was removed over compensation while the war was live, governance choosing internal peace over external survival.

The lesson

If customers hate the source of your profits, someone is already founding your replacement. Cannibalise yourself on your own schedule or be cannibalised on a rival's; the menu does not include neither. Acquisitions of the future always look expensive against the present's cash flow, which is precisely the accounting error that kills incumbents. And never fire the wartime CEO over the peacetime pay scale. Blockbuster did everything a profitable company is praised for, protecting margins, stores and fees, and the praise was the trap.
