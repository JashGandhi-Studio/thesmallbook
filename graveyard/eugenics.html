Why Did Eugenics: The Science That Filed Human Beings Fail?

When Science Gave Bigotry a Lab Coat

1945YEAR
HISTORYCAUSE
Forced sterilisations blessed by courts and universitiesTHE BURN

What happened

Eugenics was not fringe pseudoscience. For half a century it was taught at leading universities, funded by great fortunes, blessed by supreme courts and written into law on several continents: the claim that human worth is hereditary, measurable and improvable by controlling who reproduces. Francis Galton coined the term in 1883; the movement industrialised it. In the United States, more than 60,000 people were forcibly sterilised under state laws after the Supreme Court's 1927 Buck v. Bell decision, in which Oliver Wendell Holmes Jr wrote that three generations of imbeciles are enough. American model statutes and research were studied by Nazi Germany, whose 1933 sterilisation law led to some 400,000 forced sterilisations and slid into the murder of disabled people and then the Holocaust's machinery of racial classification. After 1945 the word became radioactive, though sterilisation programmes and eugenic reasoning persisted in several countries for decades, including coerced sterilisations in the 1970s and beyond. The autopsy is of an institution: elite science without ethical brakes, which did not lack data but lacked the category of human inviolability, and so filed people the way it filed specimens.

The fatal mistake

The first failure was scientific: traits like intelligence, criminality and worth were treated as simple hereditary units because measurement tools existed, a category error funded into certainty. The second was institutional capture: universities, courts and legislatures validated each other in a circle, so the consensus of the respectable replaced evidence. Third, the subjects had no power: the sterilised were poor, disabled, institutionalised and disproportionately people of colour, precisely those unable to make the filing system hear appeals. Finally, eugenics offered states a cheap substitute for social policy. It is easier to sterilise the poor than to house them, and many governments priced accordingly.

The lesson

When a science ranks human beings, ask who holds the filing cabinet and who is allowed to appeal; the answers predict the atrocities better than the data do. Consensus among elites is not a control system: Buck v. Bell was respectable, unanimous feeling science, and it was a crime with footnotes. Beware interventions that are cheaper than compassion. And remember that this was not defeated by better measurements alone; it was defeated by a moral category, human dignity, imposed on science from outside. Every field that sorts people needs that external wall, built before it needs it.
